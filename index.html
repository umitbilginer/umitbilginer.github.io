02

A shepherd first.

Story

I grew up around livestock. Our family had sheep, cattle, and chickens in rural Türkiye, and I spent years as a shepherd before I ever touched a textbook on genetics.

During my undergraduate years I worked part-time at a university research farm. What began as a way to support myself became the turning point: surrounded by breeding trials and nutrition studies, I realised genetics was the one lever that could permanently improve animal performance, welfare, and environmental impact.

That conviction took me from Akdeniz University to Aarhus University in Denmark, then to Michigan State, and now to UW–Madison, where I work on the genetics of methane emissions in dairy cattle with Francisco Peñagaricano. Along the way I picked up the industry side at Cobb-Vantress (Tyson Foods) and ABS Global (Genus plc).

Cattle · Sheep · Goats · Chickens · Quail — the principles transfer.

03

The questions I work on.

Research

The thesis — methane genetics in dairy cattle

Can we breed cows that emit less methane, without costing the farmer milk?

My PhD estimates genetic parameters for methane emission traits in US Holstein cows and builds the genomic prediction machinery to select on them — so a breeding decision made today keeps paying off, herd after herd, for free. From this work — the finding behind my poster “Less Gas, Same Glass” — roughly 30% of the variation in a cow’s methane output is genetic. Enough to select on. Enough to matter.
